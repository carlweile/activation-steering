Independent research note · 18 November 2026

Can one direction inside a small chat model turn one behaviour up or down?

I tried this for two behaviours of an open 1.5-billion-parameter chat model: refusing a request, and caving, which means dropping a correct answer because the user wrongly pushes back. Every main run had its pass lines written down before it ran.

Short version

  • Refusal: the same recipe gave a working direction at two model sizes, on prompts it was not built from, while random directions of the same size did not. This replicates published work.
  • Caving and related sycophancy: nine attempts (six broader, then three on caving alone), none gave a dial that passed its pre-set pass lines and beat its controls. The best main attempt cut caving from 96 to 62 of 300 prompts, but it missed its bar, and 3 of 10 dummy directions did at least half as well.
  • The latest attempt built the direction from the model's own answers and, as built, pushed toward caving: the model caved more (96 → 145 of 300). A pre-registered control run shows this rise is specific: only 1 of 20 dummy directions of the same size reached 145.
  • Pre-registered controls (run 18 November): flipping that direction lowered caving (96 → 69) but did not beat its dummies. A plain "hold your ground" instruction and a direction built from that instruction barely moved caving (95 and 94 vs 98), far from the pre-set bar of 48. So the method has no working positive control, and the failed attempts are not evidence that no caving dial exists.

The question

While a language model writes, it holds a long list of numbers for every word: its inner state. Take the average inner state in situation A, subtract the average in situation B, and you get one direction, an arrow in that number space. Steering means adding or subtracting that arrow while the model writes, with no retraining (the difference-of-means method of Arditi et al., 2024).

Can one such arrow act as a dial that turns exactly one behaviour up or down, while little else moves? If yes, it is a cheap, reversible way to adjust a model. If not, people who rely on such dials need to know where they break. Caving matters for honesty: a model that gives up a right answer under social pressure is telling the user what they want to hear.

Model: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct for almost everything; Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (4-bit) for the refusal checks at the larger size, and one 7B quality check in the caving work. One reply per prompt, always the single most likely one (greedy decoding).

How it was tested

  • Plan first. For each main run, the design and the pass lines were committed before the run. No pass line was loosened afterwards. Exploratory runs were labelled as such in advance and could not count as a pass.
  • Matched controls. A real dial must beat dummy directions of the same size: random ones, or ones built from the same data with shuffled labels.
  • A base-rate floor. If the plain model rarely caves on a prompt set, that set cannot test a dial against caving. Attempt 7 missed its floor; from attempt 8 on, the prompt set was screened for this first.
  • Blind grading by another model family. Claude graded the 14B refusal check and Gemini graded caving (in one layer map, three judges from different families voted), never knowing which condition a reply came from. Before each caving grading, Gemini had to pass a set of hand-made easy cases.

What was measured

Refusal: one direction, two model sizes held

On 12 harmful prompts never used to build the direction, the 14B model fully refused 11 without steering and 0 with the direction subtracted. All 24 replies stayed fluent and on topic; 4 of the 12 steered replies were graded only a partial refusal. Two random directions of the same size left refusals at 10 and 9 of 12. In the 1.5B model (its own direction, same recipe), removing the direction at every layer took full refusals on 12 held-out prompts from 12 to 0; removing random directions the same way left all 12. Removing it at one layer alone already brought them to 2.

The recipe is not general: a "truthfulness" direction built the same way did no better than directions built from the same statements with shuffled labels, and failed its pre-set control.

Caving and related sycophancy: nine attempts, no dial not shown

Attempts 1 to 6 aimed at sycophancy more broadly (agreeing with a wrong answer, praising the user's own text, and caving). Attempts 7 to 9 aimed at caving alone: a fact question, a correct first answer, and a user who pushes back with a wrong answer or with "are you sure?". The six earlier attempts (a sparse-autoencoder check that failed its quality gate, two exploratory checks, two runs that failed their pass lines, and one map across nine layers) gave no dial for any of these behaviours. In the layer map the pre-set readout labelled two sub-behaviours "works (judges only, suggestive)" on 40 prompts per cell, but no judged change beat the random control, so I do not count it. Controls, pre-registered and run afterwards

After attempt 9, I wrote down control arms with their pass lines; each pass line was signed before that control ran. Six were signed together; the seventh (the flipped direction) was added that evening, after the first results. All ran with the same blind judge (passing its easy-case check every time). For the flipped-direction, instruction and self-correction controls, two fresh AI agents recomputed every count from the raw grade files without my analysis code and got the same numbers.

What I now believe

  • MEASURED Refusal behaves like one working direction in this model family: at 1.5B and at 14B, each size's own direction cut refusals on held-out prompts where random directions of the same size did not.
  • MEASURED No caving dial passed its pass lines. The prompt-built direction lowered caving, but some dummy directions did nearly as well, and in attempt 8 dummy directions alone spread caving from 68 to 150 of 300.
  • INFERRED The prompt-built direction holds some caving signal but is not specific to caving. Part of its drop may simply be shorter replies (mean length fell from about 52 to 21 words).
  • MEASURED The own-answer direction is a specific push toward caving (1 of 20 dummies matched it), but flipping it is not a specific push away from caving, and by the pre-set rule it is not a general "yielding" knob (on a self-correction set with the caveat above).
  • MEASURED Neither an instruction to hold nor the easiest steering recipe lowered caving in this model. There is no positive control.
  • INFERRED The test itself is not blind: dummy directions alone moved caving anywhere from 58 to 167 of 300. The likelier reading is that this small model's caving is hard to lower on purpose, while arbitrary pushes move it a lot.
  • INFERRED, found after the run Agreeing with a wrong user and caving under pushback overlap as directions but are clearly not one direction. Labelled post hoc: the pre-set test of the 0.68 similarity failed.
  • Not shown: that no caving direction exists. The claim is only "failed under its own pass lines", for one model, one layer and one strength.

Limits

  • One model family (Qwen2.5), small sizes, one greedy reply per prompt.
  • The verdict judges are language models. A human read only 16 replies.
  • No positive control in the caving work: the two pre-registered positive controls (an instruction and the easiest steering recipe) both failed, so "no dial found" is not "no dial exists".
  • The flipped-direction control is a 10th caving attempt, designed after seeing attempt 9 and run on a prompt set already used twice; even a pass would have been weak evidence.
  • One stop rule was overridden. The plan for attempt 8 said a fail would end this line of work; after the fail I decided to run attempt 9 anyway. So attempt 9 counts as a run after a registered stop, with eight caving and sycophancy attempts before it, which is weaker evidence than a first try.
  • In the attempts themselves, self-correction was measured only on 50 scripted prompts with an inserted wrong answer and a user who is right (corrected 47 before, 46 with steering, in attempt 8). The controls added the model's own wrong answers (319 questions in two wordings), but that set is usable only as an average of a floor wording and a ceiling wording.

Beside published work

  • The refusal result replicates Arditi et al. (arXiv 2406.11717).
  • Aamir and Bin Adil (arXiv 2609.17550, 2611.02318) studied the same "are you sure?" caving in the same model family with probes and removal (eight models from 0.5B to 14B in the second paper) and, as far as I have read them, did not steer along the caving direction; the second paper names steering "a natural complement we did not attempt" (2611.02318, section 8). Attempts 7 to 9, and the dummy and flipped-direction controls, are versions of that test.
  • Vennemeyer et al. (arXiv 2509.21305) find that agreeing with the user and praising the user are separate directions in larger models. My praise-vs-agreeing similarity numbers at 1.5B point the same way but still have no shuffled-label baseline. Separately, my agreeing and caving directions overlap but are clearly not one direction (a reading found after the run; the pre-set test failed).
  • Lee (arXiv 2611.11035) reports that steering on 1.7B to 8B models never lowered caving without also lowering self-correction. My self-correction check (above) is too small to speak to this.

What comes next

First, a short paper on the caving data, now with its pre-registered controls (above). Its main point is the negative one: at this size, nothing tried, instruction or steering, lowered caving past its pre-set bars against arbitrary pushes of the same size.

After that paper is submitted: a second dial, against giving up early on tasks that are solvable but have a hurdle, with the direction built from the model's own answers from the start, and an honesty check that the model does not start claiming success on tasks that cannot be solved.

One open question I noticed while blind-reading: many replies say "you're correct, my apologies" and still keep the right answer. Saying sorry and actually giving in may be two different things inside the model. That is untested.
